BOSTON CHILDREN’S HOSPITAL / HEALTHCARE UX

A clearer path to ALS care resources.

A guided web app helping people with ALS and clinicians find practical resources for communication and care.

My role
UX/UI design, research, and prototyping
Focus
Information architecture, resource organization, and accessibility
Status
Design concept and prototype

The task: move from a question to a useful resource

The project explores how people with ALS and clinicians can find resources through a guided series of questions. Earlier project materials describe a web app that connects answers to relevant recommendations and resources maintained by ALS experts. My design work explored how to find, save, organize, and share those resources.

The current project materials describe the intended workflow and feature set. They do not document measured delays, a research sample, or clinical outcomes.

Organizing the experience around three tasks

01 / Find relevant information

A guided survey is proposed as a route to relevant resources. It offers more direction than an undifferentiated list, but introduces questions before the user reaches content. A next test should compare this approach with direct browsing.

02 / Save resources for later

Folders and bookmarking support repeated access. The concept includes creating, renaming, moving, sharing, and deleting folders. The design question is whether this level of organization helps clinicians retrieve information or adds unnecessary management work.

03 / Share a resource with a colleague

Sharing completes the proposed workflow. The final interaction, permissions, and delivery method need to be confirmed before presenting a production-ready handoff.

A proposed task walkthrough

This sequence describes the concept; it is not a tested interactive prototype.

  1. Find

    Use the guided survey or browse available resource categories.

  2. Save

    Bookmark a relevant resource and organize it in a folder.

  3. Share

    Choose the information to pass to another clinician.

Accessibility as a requirement to verify

The concept proposes text-size and weight controls, color choices, and contrast adjustment. These preferences should complement a usable default experience.

A next review should check keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic headings, screen-reader labels, contrast, and text reflow. The materials shown here do not establish that these checks have been completed.

Intended benefits and an evaluation plan

The intended benefits are easier resource discovery and less repeated searching. These remain design goals, rather than demonstrated improvements in clinical decisions.

  • Findability: Ask clinicians to locate a specified resource; observe completion, time, and wrong turns.
  • Retrieval: Ask them to return to a saved item and compare folders with a simpler saved list.
  • Sharing: Check whether recipients and shared content are clear before the action is completed.
  • Accessibility: Pair interface checks with testing by people who use assistive technology.
